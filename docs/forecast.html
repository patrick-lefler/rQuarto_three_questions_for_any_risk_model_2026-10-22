Question 2: Does It Beat a Simple Model?

The question

A sophisticated model earns its fee only if it beats a plain alternative. NorthPoint’s plain alternative is a 12-month rolling average of past incident counts. The question: does VendorScore forecast the bank’s monthly incident total better than that average, and does any advantage survive the deterioration in vendor patching after month 36?

Method in brief

Each month, VendorScore’s portfolio forecast is the sum of its 300 vendor scores. The benchmark is the average of the previous 12 months’ actual incident counts, computed so that month t uses only months before t. We compare both with actual incident totals over months 31 to 48, an out-of-sample, out-of-time window.

We summarise accuracy with mean squared error (MSE) and mean absolute error (MAE), and test equal accuracy with a Diebold–Mariano test using the Harvey–Leybourne–Newbold small-sample correction, from the forecastdom package. We run it twice: over months 31 to 48, and over months 37 to 48 only, after the shift. Rival approaches, such as encompassing tests or superior-predictive-ability tests, ask related questions but need longer series.

How to read the plot. Blue dots joined by a line are the actual number of incidents across all 300 vendors each month. The orange line is what VendorScore forecast. The grey dashed line is the simple 12-month average. The dotted vertical line marks when vendor patching began to slow.

What the plot tells you. Actual incidents trend upward and swing widely from month to month. The 12-month average trails the trend, forecasting too low for most of the window. VendorScore sits closer to the level of incidents but is far smoother than the actual series, so it misses most month-to-month swings. Neither forecast predicts individual spikes.

Both forecasts are too smooth to follow individual months, but they differ in level. The 12-month average under-forecast by 1.2 incidents a month on average, because it looks backwards while incidents rise. VendorScore’s average miss was 0.2.

How to read the plot. Each month we ask which forecast was closer to the truth and by how much. The line keeps a running total: it rises when VendorScore wins a month, falls when the 12-month average wins, and the height is the net advantage so far.

What the plot tells you. The line ends at 22.3 above zero, so VendorScore has a net advantage. It rises only 4.1 by month 36 and 18.2 more afterwards, and it climbs unevenly, with several losing months. A tidy upward line would be stronger evidence; this one is a mixed record with a favourable tilt.

knitr::kable(tab, digits = c(0, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 3))

How to read the table. Each row is a stretch of months. Error columns show the typical miss in incidents per month for each forecast, so smaller is better. MSE punishes large misses more heavily than MAE does. The last two columns test whether the gap is real or could be luck: a small p-value (below 0.05) means luck is an unlikely explanation.

What the table tells you. VendorScore has lower errors in both windows, but p-values of 0.235 and 0.309 are well above 0.05. Eighteen months, and twelve after the shift, are too few to separate a modest edge from chance. The result is “no detectable advantage,” not proof that the two are equal.

VendorScore’s MSE is 22% lower over the full window and 20% lower after the shift. The MAE advantage is smaller, and after the shift almost vanishes (2.03 versus 2.08). A non-significant test with n = 18 has low power, so it means no detectable advantage, not equality. The pre-shift record is also thin: months 31 to 36 contribute only six observations.

The vendor’s AUC of 0.81 is measured on its own development data. It cannot show that VendorScore stays reliable at this bank after conditions change. As a rough check, NorthPoint’s own outcomes give an AUC of 0.72 over months 31 to 48: 0.77 for months 31 to 36 and 0.70 afterwards. These come with no interval and few incidents, so a drop of a few points could be noise. Only ongoing monitoring at the bank can answer the question (§VII).

So what

On this evidence, VendorScore gets the level of incidents roughly right and the simple benchmark does not, but the advantage is statistically unproven and concentrated in a period when the benchmark lagged a rising trend. The comparison is about aggregate counts, not about whether individual vendors are ranked correctly.

The April 2026 interagency Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management treats out-of-sample and out-of-time testing and benchmarking as part of model testing (§IV) and ongoing monitoring (§V). The cumulative-differential chart is the kind of metric that fits there: it turns “is the model still earning its place?” into a line a committee can watch. This is partial validation: it tests outputs, not the proprietary method.

Committee action

Sound practice suggests benchmarking every vendor model against a named simple alternative, out of sample and out of time, and adding the loss differential to ongoing monitoring so that deterioration triggers review.
